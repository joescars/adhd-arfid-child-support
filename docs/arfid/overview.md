# ARFID: an evidence-oriented starting point

ARFID stands for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder. It is different from ordinary picky eating and can involve avoiding foods, eating a very limited range, or not eating enough for reasons such as sensory sensitivity, low interest in eating, or fear of aversive consequences.[1][2]

ARFID can affect nutrition, growth, energy, social participation, and family life. The same outward behavior can have different causes, so a clinician should assess the whole picture rather than relying on a food-count checklist.[1][2]

## What to document for a care team

- the range and categories of foods and drinks accepted
- changes over time and any sudden losses of previously accepted foods
- sensory features: texture, smell, temperature, appearance, brand, presentation
- fear or past experiences involving choking, vomiting, pain, or allergic reactions
- appetite, fatigue, constipation, nausea, or pain
- meal duration, distress, school impact, and social avoidance
- growth, hydration, and any known nutrient concerns as assessed by clinicians

## What not to do from a webpage

Do not force bites, shame the child, secretly alter food, remove all safe foods, or begin supplements or restrictive diets without professional guidance. Feeding treatment should be individualized and coordinated with medical, nutrition, and mental-health needs.

## Finding the right help

Ask the pediatrician what local services have experience with ARFID or pediatric feeding difficulties. Depending on the child's needs, the team may include a pediatrician, registered dietitian, psychologist or psychiatrist, occupational therapist, speech-language pathologist, and/or feeding program. Availability and professional roles vary by location.
