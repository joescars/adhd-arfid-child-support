# ARFID: an evidence-oriented starting point

ARFID stands for avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder. It involves restricted amount or variety of food related to factors such as low interest in eating, sensory characteristics, or fear of adverse consequences. A food preference or short checklist cannot establish ARFID; assessment belongs with qualified clinicians.[1][2]

ARFID may be associated with consequences such as growth or nutrition concerns or interference with daily and social life. Pediatric feeding disorder (PFD) and swallowing difficulty (dysphagia) describe different clinical concerns; they may overlap with ARFID but are not interchangeable diagnoses. These sources provide definitions and professional guidance, not a way for families to diagnose a child or evidence that a particular treatment works.[1][2]

## What a parent can do now

If useful, a caregiver may make a brief factual note of what changed, foods and fluids the child currently accepts, symptoms or distress, and effects on daily life. The purpose is to organize a future conversation, not score intake; this is a low-burden option that needs no special equipment. Stop if it increases conflict or delays care. Seek prompt clinical guidance if intake, hydration, growth, swallowing, or distress worsens; use [red flags and urgent help](../safety/red-flags-and-urgent-help.md) for escalation. Do not remove accepted foods or change diet or treatment based on this page.

## What to document for a care team

- the range and categories of foods and drinks accepted
- changes over time and any sudden losses of previously accepted foods
- sensory features: texture, smell, temperature, appearance, brand, presentation
- fear or past experiences involving choking, vomiting, pain, or allergic reactions
- appetite, fatigue, constipation, nausea, or pain
- meal duration, distress, school impact, and social avoidance
- growth, hydration, and any known nutrient concerns as assessed by clinicians

## What not to do from a webpage

Do not force bites, shame the child, secretly alter food, remove all safe foods, or begin supplements or restrictive diets without professional guidance. Feeding treatment should be individualized and coordinated with medical, nutrition, and mental-health needs.

## Finding the right help

Start with the child's pediatrician or primary-care clinician, who can assess medical concerns and coordinate referrals; this does not alone establish ARFID. A mental-health clinician may assess ARFID-related eating concerns; a registered dietitian may assess nutrition; a speech-language pathologist may assess swallowing/feeding skills within their competence; an occupational therapist may assess relevant participation or sensory-motor needs. Roles, pediatric experience, referral rules, insurance, and availability vary. Ask about experience with the child's age and ARFID or feeding/swallowing concerns, who coordinates the team, and what each professional does not assess.

## Questions to take to a clinician

- What could explain the eating change, and what should be assessed first?
- Do growth, hydration, nutrition, pain, anxiety, or chewing/swallowing safety need assessment?
- Which professional is the right first referral, and who will coordinate care?
- What changes should prompt a call before the next appointment?

## Red flags and escalation

For emergency signs, prompt clinician contact, and routine follow-up, use the [red flags and urgent help guide](../safety/red-flags-and-urgent-help.md). If a child has trouble breathing or swallowing, cannot keep fluids down, faints, is unusually weak or drowsy, or appears to be in immediate danger, seek local emergency help. Contact the child's clinician promptly for worsening intake, hydration, growth, or distress; do not wait to complete notes or try food exploration.
