# Finding and understanding clinical trials

Clinical trials can help families learn about research, but a registry entry is not an endorsement, proof of safety, or recommendation that a child should participate. Eligibility, location, language, travel, time, risks, and current recruitment must be confirmed with the study team and the child’s clinician.

## Where to search

- [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov/): international registry with searches by condition, age, location, recruitment status, and study identifier.
- [WHO ICTRP Search Portal](https://trialsearch.who.int/): international search across participating registry networks.
- [EU Clinical Trials Information System](https://euclinicaltrials.eu/ctis-public/search?lang=en): European Union and European Economic Area medicinal-product trials.

Search terms may include **ARFID**, **avoidant/restrictive food intake disorder**, **pediatric feeding disorder**, **feeding and swallowing**, **ADHD children**, **parent training**, or **sleep ADHD**. Try synonyms and check the age and recruitment filters. ClinicalTrials.gov and WHO ICTRP provide registry search information, not clinical recommendations.[1][2]

The EU registry and FDA participation guidance serve different jurisdictions and purposes; read the study record and discuss participation with the child’s clinician.[3][4]

## Questions before contacting a study

- Is the study recruiting now, and when was the status last updated?
- What ages and diagnoses are eligible?
- Does the study require a confirmed diagnosis or accept a referral?
- What visits, travel, language, assessments, or home tasks are required?
- What are the known risks, burdens, alternatives, and compensation rules?
- Who pays for usual care and what happens if the child becomes medically unstable?
- Can participation be stopped, and who remains responsible for routine clinical care?

## What a registry entry cannot tell you by itself

A registry record does not prove that an intervention works, that a listing is current, or that a study is appropriate for a particular child. For example, a small ARFID parent-training pilot illustrates why age, study size, design, and preliminary status matter.[5] Do not stop treatment, delay urgent care, or change medication to qualify for a study.
